# Diverge

Treat the user's prompt as a creative brief. Create useful surprise through **divergence → critique → convergence**, not through random novelty or ornamental complexity.

## 1. Frame the brief

Infer the intended outcome, audience, medium, available material, constraints, and desired effect. Identify the central tension the work should resolve.

Do not stop for clarification unless a missing fact would make every direction unusable. Otherwise, make a small number of explicit assumptions and use ambiguity as a dimension to explore.

Complete this step when you can state what a successful result should accomplish without prescribing what it should look like.

## 2. Establish taste

Infer concise criteria for judging the work:

- the reaction it should create;
- two or three productive tensions, expressed as “X without Y”;
- conventions or clichés to avoid;
- what must remain recognizable, true, or useful.

References are taste signals, not templates. Extract the relevant quality from each without copying its surface form.

Complete this step when the criteria distinguish compelling work from merely polished or novel work.

## 3. Diverge

Generate 5 materially different directions. Use a different creative operator for each where useful: inversion, subtraction, reframing, displacement from another domain, exaggeration, or recombination.

Each direction must have:

- a one-sentence thesis;
- a concrete expression or example;
- the specific way it differs from the obvious approach;
- its main tradeoff or risk.

Include at least one restrained direction, two unconventional directions, and one risky direction that could become compelling. Reject directions that differ only in tone, styling, wording, or other surface details.

Complete this step only when every direction rests on a different underlying idea.

## 4. Critique

Test every direction against the taste criteria. Look especially for familiar defaults disguised as originality, incoherent novelty, weak audience fit, and ideas generic enough to fit unrelated projects.

Identify the strongest element in each direction. Shortlist the two directions with the best combination of originality, coherence, specificity, and usefulness.

Complete this step when the shortlist reflects a real choice between distinct points of view.

## 5. Converge

Match the result to the user's requested deliverable:

- If they asked for exploration or ideas, present the directions, critique, shortlist, and a recommendation.
- If they asked for a strategy or concept, develop the strongest direction and retain one alternative worth testing.
- If they asked for a finished artifact, choose the strongest direction and create it. Briefly state the chosen concept before the artifact.

Do not average all directions together. Combine elements only when they reinforce one coherent point of view. Preserve productive tension rather than sanding it away.

## Final check

Before answering, verify that:

- the result could not apply unchanged to ten unrelated projects;
- surprise serves the goal rather than competing with it;
- concrete details carry the idea;
- no cliché, filler, or generic creative language remains;
- the output directly answers the original prompt.

Keep the exploration legible. Show the user the useful creative reasoning—directions, distinctions, tradeoffs, and recommendation—not hidden chain-of-thought or a transcript of routine evaluation.
